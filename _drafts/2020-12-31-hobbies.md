Where does all of my free time go? A list of non-work things that I've spent my time on throughout the year:

- __Building a music looper (["loop-baby"](https://github.com/mobeets/loop-baby))__ [Oct. 2019 - Jan. 2020]: The first true hobby I had since the boys were born was working on a music looper (the "loop-baby"). The goal was to make an affordable, open-source digital loop station that allows midi syncing (e.g., to sync your sampled loop to a drum machine). This involved a) getting trained to use CMU's laser cutter, b) designing and fabricating the physical device (using a Raspberry Pi), and c) writing a software interface for [Sooperlooper](http://essej.net/sooperlooper/) to control the device. After spending nearly every night for two months straight working on this project, I got a working prototype. Sadly, thanks to COVID-19, I no longer have access to the laser cutter, which means I never got to print a final, perfected version. But one day I will finish this thing!
- __Creating a board game (["Gourmant"](https://mobeets.github.io/gourmant-js/))__ [Jan. - Apr. 2020]: Jess and I wanted to create a tile-placing game like Carcassonne, but with a more hierarchical goal structure like in Dominion. Our game, which we called "Gourmant", felt _very_ close to actually being decent. But as with so many of our other attempts at making a board game, we eventually realized the mechanics were broken in a way that made continuing work on it seem totally pointless. Or maybe we just got burnt out on the idea.
- __Simulating disease spread and seasonality ([simulator](https://mobeets.github.io/disease-spread/))__ [Mar. 2020]: When the pandemic began, like many simulator-savvy people I became interested in models of disease spread. In this simulator I made, I was interested in generating "seasonality" (i.e., oscillations) in the number of people who have a given disease over time, similar to how flu gets worse every fall/winter.
- __Music tuning theory__ [Jun. 2020]: I've been learning so much about music theory (and music history) from Adam Neely's youtube channel. [This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TYhPAbsIqA8) video in particular, about something called a "comma pump," got me interested in understanding musical tuning. I wrote a blog post about what I learned, though I haven't published it. The blog post is about how with any given tuning system, there's inevitably a trade-off between maintaining perfectly harmonious intervals (e.g., a 3:2 ratio) and avoiding inconsistencies in how different intervals are defined. The tuning system used in Western music, for example, prioritizes the latter.
- __What's a busy beaver number?__ [Sep. 2020]: After seeing a [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X3l0fPHZja8) by Numberphile, and reading Scott Aaronson's ["The Busy Beaver Frontier"](https://www.scottaaronson.com/papers/bb.pdf), I got really into trying to understand the Busy Beaver sequence. I then wrote yet another blog post about what I learned that I have yet to post anywhere.
- __Learning to play "Autumn Leaves" on piano__ [Jul. 2020]: Learning to play piano is something that's been a New Year's resolution for me basically every year since high school. But this year, I finally made some substantial progress. I learned to play "Autumn Leaves" with open voicings (following [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nol5v6hcrLM) video), which was very difficult but also satisfying.
- __Learning to play John Lennon's "Imagine" and Harry Nilsson's "Gotta Get Up" on piano__ [Oct. 2020]: Learning "Autumn Leaves" taught me to be able to switch between chords more effortlessly than I'd been able to before (even chords with sharps!). This was especially useful for "Gotta Get Up." When I learned that even Jess struggled to play those chord changes, I think I finally realized that I had really made a lot of progress with my piano skills.
- __Adding google ngrams-like functionality to my journal__ [Nov. 2020]: I've been writing a daily journal for the last 12 years or so. One thing I really wanted to know was how often I've used different words over time. Using plotly for visualization, I ended up building my own version of google's [ngram viewer](https://books.google.com/ngrams) for my journal entries. Some of my favorite plots were i) how often I curse over time (monotonic decreasing); ii) how often I mention various pets I've lived with; and iii) whether I write about Robin or Jordan more often (basically equal!).
- __Paper Trails #10: [Public Choice](https://mobeets.github.io/paper-trails/2020/11/02/choice/)__ [Nov. 2020]: Right before Election Day, I sent out my first Paper Trails email in a long time, about some concepts in "public choice." I'd written it two years prior (!), so it needed some updating.
- __How often do we actually use the random things in our drawers?__ [Nov. 2020]: Everyone, I'm assuming, has a drawer of odds-and-ends somewhere in their house: a drawer that holds things like scissors, rubberbands, spare keys, old batteries, etc. For each item in the drawer, write down roughly how many times in the last year you've used it. Now, what's the distribution of those times, across all items? I made Jess help me actually go through our utility drawer and try to answer this question. The answer: Poisson, roughly. (Go figure.) The trick to guessing this answer involves asking the question in the right way! We'd first asked "When was the last time we used this item?", which is _not_ Poisson, but something like 1/Poisson.
- __Secret Santa code__ [Dec. 2020]: This year, my high school friends and I had a virtual "Secret Santa," with assignments generated and emailed by some code I wrote back in 2008. The code needed a little dusting off and reorganizing.
- __Extrinsic vs. intrinsic rewards in reinforcement learning (RL)__ [Dec. 2020]: Research-wise, I've been doing a lot of thinking about the degree to which the brain might perform reinforcement learning (RL). In RL, an agent learns to maximize a scalar quantity called "reward." Is it reasonable to assume that when we train an animal to perform a given behavior that we can actually control what the animal's reward signal is? While thinking about this, I was particularly inspired by a talk by Deepak Pathak about [curiosity-driven RL](https://pathak22.github.io/noreward-rl/), and a great [paper](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4491543/) by Wolfram Schultz on what the brain's reward functions might be.
- __Training an RL agent to play a video game using intrinsic rewards__ [Dec. 2020]: My first goal was to train an agent in the [OpenAI gym](https://gym.openai.com/) to learn a task via curiosity-driven RL. But, having never really trained an RL agent to do _anything_, I first starting with the basics, solving cartpole with Q-learning (in a colab notebook). My next goal was to solve an Atari game like Breakout--no, Pong--using an evolutionary strategy--no, a DQN--no, policy gradients. Well, long story short: I never succeeded. Solving these tasks seemed like it was going to take tens of hours of training--not minutes--and I started feeling impatient. But I did learn a lot about the basics of RL!
- __Rubik's cube__ [currently]: Revisiting my Rubik's cube has been on my mind ever since seeing the amazing documentary _The Speed Cubers_. I hadn't solved one in about four years, and relearning always involves remembering how to solve the third layer. My goals are a) to learn [CFOP](https://www.speedsolving.com/wiki/index.php/CFOP_method), the most popular solving method; and b) to bring my best time down from 1:40 to 1:00.
